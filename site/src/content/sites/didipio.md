## What the images show

The first "before" composite comes from the 2005–2007 dry seasons, before the 2008 demolitions and road
works. Dinkidi Hill is still there, under forest and farms. In the 2026 composite the hill is gone. In its
place is the open pit, the ringed benches at the centre, surrounded by bare waste rock, the processing plant
and the tailings storage facility. Switch on the magenta layer to see every pixel that went from green
vegetation to bare ground or water.

The Sentinel-2 pair starts after the pit was dug. It shows what has changed since then, mostly at the edges
of the tailings area.

The company says the mine occupies about 334 hectares. The footprint that Tang & Werner traced on satellite
imagery, working independently of the company, comes out close to that.

## What residents say

About 4,000 Indigenous people live in Didipio and neighbouring Alimit. The land was first settled by the
Bugkalot. Tuwali and Ayangan families from Ifugao and Kalanguya and Ibaloy families from Benguet moved in
from the 1950s. Mongabay reports that because of this history, residents are not regarded as ancestral
domain holders. That keeps them from invoking free, prior and informed consent under the Indigenous
Peoples' Rights Act.

Residents told Mongabay in 2021 that at least a dozen water pumps and springs near the mine had dried up, and
that farmland had been abandoned for lack of irrigation. They attribute this to the pit and the underground
workings. Satellite imagery cannot confirm or rule this out. The area is part of the headwaters of the
Addalam River, which feeds the Cagayan River and irrigates rice land downstream in Isabela and Cagayan.
