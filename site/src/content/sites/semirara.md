## What the images show

There is no "before mining" image. Unong had been producing coal for five years by 1988. In the 1988–90
Landsat composite, the north end of the island is brown grass and scrub, ringed by pale reef flats. The
north coast then lay about 2.5–3 km south of where it is now.

By 2026 the northern third of the island is one mining complex. It holds pale benches and waste dumps,
dark pit floors and a green pit lake, and most of it stands where there was sea in 1988. Turn on the
water-change layer: lime marks about 2,370 ha that went from water to land between the two images, and
nearly all of it is this new ground along the north and east coasts. The 2016 DENR audit identified coastal
reclamation near Molave that destroyed coral reef. The images show how far the shore moved. They do not show
what the new land is made of or which approval covered it.

The Sentinel-2 pair shows the same growth continuing after 2016–18: about 860 ha more of new land along
the north-west and east shores, and a pit in the north-west that filled with water (blue). Most of the
magenta (green to bare) lies along the southern edge of the mine complex, where it has spread into scrub
and farmland.

## Who operates here

Semirara Mining and Power Corporation, controlled by DMCI Holdings, operates the island's surface coal
mines under Coal Operating Contract No. 5. The contract was first awarded in 1977, assigned to the company
in 1980 and extended in 2008 to July 14, 2027. The DOE still listed COC No. 5 in development and production
in 2025. As of September 2026, no further term had been awarded.

The pits are successive parts of one mining complex. Unong produced coal from 1983 to 2000. Panian
replaced it in 1999 and closed after depletion in September 2016. Narra and Molave began commercial
production later in 2016. Narra resumed in 2022 after work to address water seepage. SMPC closed Molave
early after a November 2023 mudflow changed the pit and made its remaining reserves uneconomic. Its 2025
filing says Narra and the new Acacia area are now the two active pits.

## The Panian collapses

On February 13, 2013, part of Panian's west wall collapsed. Five workers were confirmed dead, five were
missing and three were rescued. The company stopped work in the affected area. On July 17, 2015, a second
collapse at the pit's northern edge killed nine workers. The DOE suspended mining and DENR's written order
allowed only search, rescue and rehabilitation at Panian. The DOE permitted operations to resume that
September after saying the company had substantially complied with its safety conditions.

These were regulatory responses to fatal mine accidents, not court findings that the company committed a
crime.

## What residents and advocates say

In 2009, residents of Sitio Bigo told GMA News that waste they believed came from the coal-washing plant
was reaching the sea, covering parts of the coastline and mangroves and affecting fishing, shell gathering
and seaweed farming. A 2017 Interaksyon report attributed to residents and a climate-justice group the claim
that black coal particles had appeared in seaweed farms near the washing facility, destroying or forcing the
relocation of farms. These are attributed accounts, not findings of legal liability.

In January 2026, Catholic and Caritas leaders opposed another coal-contract extension. They said mining had
damaged coastal ecosystems and traditional livelihoods, and cited seaweed farmers who said coal dust had
settled on their crops. They called for livelihood restoration, compensation and a transition away from coal.

## What the government and company say

The 2016 DENR audit presents a mixed record. Its summary called the Molave expansion technically sound and
reported compliance with environmental laws. The same report identified coastal reclamation near Molave
that caused coral-reef destruction and disturbance to the marine ecosystem, and said agreed mitigation
measures should be followed. It recorded that the company used recycled water to suppress dust on coal and
roads, contained and reused coal-washing water, and decommissioned the washing plant in November 2015.

In 2025, DENR granted an amended environmental compliance certificate that expanded the approved project
area to 5,221.75 hectares and allowed up to 20 million metric tons of coal a year through 2027. In September
2026, DOE terminated the bidding round that included the Semirara coal areas, citing stakeholder concerns,
water seepage, other island developments and legal considerations. It said it would reassess the award
framework; that cancellation did not itself extend SMPC's existing contract.
